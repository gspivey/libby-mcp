# Nonfiction Taste Profile

A running record of what makes a nonfiction book *valuable* vs. merely
*interesting* — built from actual reading evidence, not theory.

Two uses:
1. **Reader's filter** — predict whether a book will pay off before sinking hours in.
2. **Writer's spec** — the standard to hold a book to if I write one.

Last updated: 2026-07-22

---

## Part 1 — The Evidence (books judged, with reasons)

### Landed — high value per hour

| Book | Why it worked |
|---|---|
| **Atomic Habits** (Clear) | Every chapter converts to a behavior you can run tomorrow. Framework first, evidence in support — not the reverse. |
| **Thinking, Fast and Slow** (Kahneman) | Dense, but the density *is* the payload. Named, reusable concepts (System 1/2, anchoring, availability). Changes how you see your own reasoning. |
| **Becoming Trader Joe** (Coulombe) | **The proof case for narrative done right.** It's entirely a story — a memoir — and it works, because the story *is* the framework. You watch him reason through real constraints, so the decision logic is visible and portable. Dated (1960s–80s retail) and still valuable: you can see the framework and imagine how to modernize it. |

### Interesting but insufficient — good idea, thin conversion

| Book | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Primal Intelligence** (Fletcher) | Genuinely novel thesis, but less actionable than Atomic Habits. Idea-rich, application-poor. |
| **Wanting** (Burgis) | Mimetic desire is a real lens and personally useful — but repetitive, retrofitted case studies, and the business applications drift manipulation-adjacent. Returned early. |
| **Plays Well with Others** (Barker) | Engaging writer *and* narrator. But the structure is: complicate a piece of conventional wisdom → cite studies → short "so what" at the end. Takeaways thin relative to runtime. Interesting ≠ useful. |
| **Millionaire Milestones** | Redundant with what I already knew and already do. |

### Bounced — actively didn't work

| Book | Failure mode |
|---|---|
| **Smarter Faster Better** (Duhigg) | Story → story → belated point. The stories don't *carry* a framework, they postpone one. Abandoned at ch. 3. |
| **Gladwell/Duhigg style generally** | Dealbreaker — but note *why*: not because they tell stories, because the story is the product and the insight is decoration. |

## Part 2 — Extracted Traits

### What makes a nonfiction book GOOD (for me)

- **Narrative as the *vehicle*, not the payload.** Story is welcome — often the
  best delivery mechanism there is. The test is whether the story *carries* a
  transferable concept or *stands in* for one. Trader Joe: the story is how you
  see the framework. Duhigg: the story is instead of one.
- **Framework-first, evidence-second.** Give me the reusable structure, then support it. Not: 40 pages of story, then a sentence of point.
- **Visible reasoning beats current examples.** A dated framework is still
  valuable if you can see *why* the author decided what they decided — you can
  port the logic forward and modernize it yourself. Obsolescence of examples is
  forgivable; opacity of reasoning is not.
- **Named, portable concepts.** "System 1 / System 2." "Habit stacking." Things I can carry into a conversation a year later.
- **Practitioner authority.** The author did the thing, or did the research. Not a journalist assembling other people's anecdotes.
- **Convertible to action.** After each chapter I should be able to name something I'd *do* differently — or a lens I'd *apply* differently. One of the two, explicitly.
- **Honest about limits.** Says where the evidence is thin, where the framework breaks. Hedging *increases* trust.
- **Density respected.** Short is fine. Long is fine if the length is load-bearing. Padding is not.

### What makes it BAD

- **Anecdote as *substitute* for argument.** Not "stories are bad" — stories are
  great. But a vivid story is not evidence, and three vivid stories are not three
  pieces of evidence. If removing the story removes the claim, there was no claim.
- **Citation density mistaken for rigor.** Lots of studies cited ≠ strong claims. Much of the pop social-psych canon is replication-crisis-era. A book that leans on study-count rather than effect-size or replication is performing rigor, not doing it.
- **Retrofitted case studies.** Picking a thesis, then finding companies/people who fit it. Survivorship bias dressed as insight.
- **Complicate-then-shrug.** Spend a chapter overturning conventional wisdom, land on "it's complicated." Interesting, useless.
- **Ethically slippery applications.** Frameworks pitched as ways to influence people without their knowledge. Fails the filter even when intellectually interesting. (Killed *Wanting*'s business half.)
- **Redundancy with the reader's existing practice.** Not the book's fault, but a real filter — sample early.

### Engaging ≠ Valuable (the key distinction)

The most important finding so far. **Barker and Duhigg are both excellent writers.**
Engaging prose and warm narration are *orthogonal* to whether a book pays off.
Charm can carry a book for 2 hours before you notice nothing has transferred.

> **Diagnostic:** at the 25–30% mark, ask — *what would I do or see differently
> because of this book?* If the honest answer is "nothing yet, but I'm enjoying
> it," the ratio won't improve. Release it.

## Part 3 — The Early-Warning Checklist (reader's filter)

Run at ~1 hour / ~25%:

- [ ] Can I name the book's central framework in one sentence?
- [ ] Has at least one idea already changed something I'd do or notice?
- [ ] Is the author a practitioner or primary researcher, or a narrator of others' work?
- [ ] Are stories *carrying* a claim, or *substituting* for one? (If I summarized
      the story in one line, would a transferable idea survive?)
- [ ] Does the author acknowledge any limits of their own thesis?
- [ ] Would a 40-page version lose anything?

3+ fails → release it. Re-queue later if curious; the hold is free.

## Part 4 — Spec for the Book I'd Write

The intersection of what I'm actually like and what's actually valuable.

**My unfair advantages as an author:**
- Practitioner, not observer — systems engineer + bootstrapped founder building
  a real product with real revenue pressure. Primary-source material.
- Comfortable with technical depth *and* able to explain it. Rust/DPDK,
  distributed systems, and API reverse-engineering are not abstractions to me.
- Ethical filter runs by default — I reject frameworks that feel manipulative
  even when they're interesting. That's a differentiator in the business genre.
- Bias toward feasibility-before-solution and debugging over guessing. That's a
  *method*, and methods are what readers can actually reuse.

**Therefore the book should:**
- Be **framework-first**, with my own work as the worked example — not a
  survey of other people's companies.
- Deliver **named, portable concepts** a reader can cite from memory.
- Be **honest about failure** — the wrong hypotheses, the dead ends. (The most
  useful part of any technical story is what *didn't* work and how you found out.)
- Convert **every chapter to an action or a lens**, stated explicitly.
- Be **as short as the argument requires.** Resist the 250-page trade-nonfiction
  convention if the idea is a 120-page idea.
- **Use narrative deliberately** — it's the best delivery mechanism I've got, and
  the build stories (reverse-engineering an undocumented API, the wrong
  hypotheses, the fix) are real. But every story must carry a portable concept
  out with it. Refuse the anecdote-as-*substitute* move, even though it sells.
- **Make the reasoning visible enough to outlive the examples.** Assume the
  specific tools are obsolete in five years; write so a reader can still see the
  decision logic and modernize it. That's the Trader Joe standard.

**Candidate territory** (intersection of experience + genuine reader need):
- Building and shipping solo as an engineer-founder — the parts that aren't
  code and aren't VC advice.
- Debugging as a general-purpose thinking method (hypothesis → test → discard),
  applied outside software.
- Reverse-engineering undocumented systems as a practical skill.

**The anti-goal:** a charming, well-narrated book that people enjoy and forget.
